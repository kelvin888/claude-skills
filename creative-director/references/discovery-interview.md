# Discovery interview — question bank & challenge patterns

The goal of discovery isn't to fill a form. It's to extract a sharp, provable point of view
the whole experience can hang on. Ask in **focused rounds of 5–8**, grouped by theme, and
*react* to answers before moving on. When an answer is vague, cliché, or unproven, challenge
it on the spot (see the bottom of this file) — that interrogation is where the quality comes
from.

You rarely need every question. Read the room: a solo founder building a portfolio needs a
lighter touch than a funded startup relaunching. Pick the questions that move you toward the
exit criteria.

---

## Round 1 — Business & purpose
- What is the product, in one plain sentence a stranger would understand?
- Why does it exist? What problem does it kill?
- Why now? What changed in the world that makes this the moment?
- What's the single most important action a visitor should take?
- What happens after they take it — what's the real business outcome?

## Round 2 — Audience
- Who is the **one** primary visitor? (Not "everyone." One person.)
- What were they doing 30 seconds before they landed here? What mood?
- What frustrates them about the current options?
- What would make them stop scrolling and pay attention?
- What do they already believe that we can use — or have to overturn?
- Are they on a laptop with time, or a phone in a hurry? (This decides ambition vs. weight.)

## Round 3 — Positioning & difference
- Who are the real competitors — including the "do nothing" option?
- What's the one thing that's true about you that competitors would struggle to copy?
- What should a visitor think after **15 seconds**? After they leave?
- If we deleted your logo, would anyone know it was you? What makes it unmistakably yours?

## Round 4 — Brand personality
Avoid "what colors do you want?" — that's an output, not an input. Instead:
- If your company walked into a room, how would people describe it? (fearless, playful,
  luxurious, futuristic, human, technical, irreverent, precise…)
- Name a brand in a *totally different industry* whose vibe you'd kill for. Why that one?
- What would your brand never do or say? (The "never" list defines you as much as the "always".)

## Round 5 — Emotion (most sites skip this entirely)
- What single emotion should a visitor feel? (curiosity, awe, confidence, calm, urgency,
  desire, mischief, optimism…) Pick **one** primary; secondaries are fine but one leads.
- When in the journey should that feeling peak?
- What feeling must they absolutely **not** have? (confused, overwhelmed, sold-to, bored?)

## Round 6 — Story
- What story are we telling? (Not "what sections" — what's the arc?)
- Who's the hero — the user or the product? (Usually the user; the product is the guide.)
- What's the transformation? Where are they before, where after?
- Is there a tension, a mystery, or a reveal we can build toward instead of dumping
  everything in the hero?

## Round 7 — Practical constraints (ask, don't assume)
- Timeline and rough ambition level — a polished one-pager or a flagship experience?
- Any existing brand assets, fonts, colors, 3D, or footage to use or honor?
- Accessibility / performance / device requirements? (A heavy WebGL hero is a choice with
  costs — surface it now, not after it's built.)
- Who needs to approve this, and what will *they* judge it on?

## Challenge patterns — push back before you accept

A real director doesn't transcribe answers; they pressure-test them. Reach for these when an
answer is too soft to design from. Be warm but relentless — you're on the user's side, which
is exactly why you won't let a weak brief through.

| When the user says… | Push back with… |
|---|---|
| "We're innovative / cutting-edge." | "Everyone claims that. What specific capability would a competitor struggle to replicate? Show me the proof, not the adjective." |
| "We use AI." | "What does that let a user *do* that was impossible before? Describe the exact moment they feel it." |
| "Make it modern / clean / premium." | "Premium how — Aesop-restrained or Nike-explosive? Those are opposite builds. Send me three references that feel right and one that's close-but-wrong." |
| "Our audience is everyone." | "Pick the one person who, if we move them, the rest follow. Design for them; the others come along." |
| "We want it to pop / wow." | "Wow at what exact moment? Wow is a result, not a brief. What do they see, and what do they feel a half-second later?" |
| "Just make it look like [famous site]." | "What specifically — the typography, the scroll feel, the restraint, the color? We borrow a *principle*, not a skin." |
| "We do a bit of everything." | "Then the homepage will say nothing. What's the one thing we lead with so the rest has a frame?" |

When you get a real answer, **reflect it back** sharper than they said it and confirm:
"So the one feeling is *quiet confidence*, the hero is the operator not us, and the proof is
[X]. Right?" Locking these in is what makes everything downstream coherent.

---

## Exit criteria (don't leave Phase 1 until you can fill these)

State each in one sentence, concretely:

- **Product:** _______
- **Primary audience (one person):** _______
- **Single most differentiating truth (provable):** _______
- **One emotion a visitor must feel:** _______
- **The story / arc:** _______

If any line is still generic, you have more interviewing to do.
